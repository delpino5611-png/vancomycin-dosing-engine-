# The Differentiable Dosing Engine: Solving the Vancomycin Dose Across the Renal Spectrum

*I am a pharmacist who builds predictive models. This is a model informed precision dosing engine built from composed, differentiable modules, and I validated every regimen it produces.*

## Why I built this

In a prior role I managed vancomycin dosing by hand for elderly patients in long term care. The facilities drew a trough, called the pharmacy after the fourth dose, and I adjusted the next regimen from that single number. Those patients were largely renally impaired, exactly where standard weight based dosing fails hardest. And the trough itself was never guaranteed. The home had no lab of its own, so when the outside lab could not make it or the draw fell on a weekend, no level was taken at all, and we were left holding the dose or guessing on a frail renal patient. Waiting for steady state and titrating by hand off one timed trough, on the days the trough even arrived, is the workflow this engine improves on. I also build predictive models. Most dosing tools are built by engineers who never dosed a drug, or described by clinicians who cannot build. I am in the overlap, so I built the tool I would have wanted then: one that targets exposure from the first dose instead of chasing a trough after the fourth.

The dose is where errors concentrate. Medication error is a leading source of preventable hospital harm, and by type the largest single category is not the wrong drug or route but the wrong amount: wrong dosage and overdose together account for close to 77 percent of medication errors [M1]. Bedside smart pumps block bad infusion rates but cannot compute the right dose for a patient whose kidneys break the standard regimen [M1]. Vancomycin is the ideal proving ground: a narrow therapeutic index antibiotic dosed daily in every hospital, where too little means treatment failure and too much means kidney injury.

## The gap: the field asked for this, and the best attempt only reasons

Gettman's review calls directly for AI assisted dosing software that ingests patient data, computes personalized parameters such as clearance and volume, simulates regimens, and recommends an optimal dose with Bayesian updating in sparse data settings; the methods exist but are underused at the point of care [R1]. The strongest recent attempt is Salmeron's agentic architecture, where a language model agent reasons qualitatively toward a dose and outputs something like a "reduced dose" [R2]. It reasons about the dose; it does not solve for it. There is no gradient and no optimizer, so it cannot invert the pharmacokinetics to find the regimen that hits a target. That is what a differentiable simulation supplies.

## How it works

I built the drug's physiology as three separate, composable, differentiable modules, so the computer can calculate how a change in dose ripples through to final exposure and solve for the regimen instead of guessing.

The first maps covariates (age, weight, height, serum creatinine, sex) to vancomycin parameters through published formulas: Devine ideal body weight, adjusted body weight as the best clinical correlate [V1], Cockcroft and Gault creatinine clearance [C1], the Matzke relationship Ke = 0.00083 x CrCl + 0.0044 [V5], volume of distribution at 0.7 L/kg, and clearance as their product. Vancomycin is roughly 90 percent renally cleared, so creatinine clearance is the dominant driver. The second is a one compartment intermittent infusion model driven to steady state by superposition of prior doses, returning a differentiable trapezoidal AUC over 24 hours plus the steady state peak and trough, verified against the identity that AUC equals daily dose divided by clearance. The third is the objective: a smooth loss on AUC/MIC 400 to 600, the modern consensus that replaced trough only dosing [V4], with MIC an explicit input defaulting to 1 mg/L [V4] and one sided penalties on an excessive peak and on troughs above 20 or below 10 mg/L. At an MIC of 2 or higher the target would demand an AUC of 800 to 1200, futile and nephrotoxic, so the engine flags an alternative agent rather than a higher dose [V4].

Because vancomycin does not reach target for three to four doses, the engine returns two phases: an empiric loading dose of 25 mg/kg actual body weight capped at 3 g [V4][V2], then a gradient optimized maintenance regimen snapped to the clinical schedule. An Adam optimizer descends on a bounded dose, a deliberately non differentiable guardrail layer applies the discrete safety rules and selects the safe regimen, and a MAP Bayesian fitter individualizes a patient from as few as two measured levels by differentiating the level predictions back through the served model.

I am candid about where the gradient earns its keep. For a fixed one compartment structure the forward dose is near closed form and a small search would find it. It becomes indispensable in the Bayesian inverse, where recovering parameters from sparse, noisy levels (a four parameter inverse in the two compartment model) is a fit automatic differentiation solves far better than grid search, and in composition, where a single gradient of the final loss flows back to a covariate such as serum creatinine across three separately served modules with no hand derived chain rule, which is what lets me swap a model or objective without replumbing anything.

## What it does

Standard dosing of 15 mg/kg every 12 hours, applied without regard to renal function, fails at both ends. For a young patient with augmented clearance (CrCl 158 mL/min) it gives an AUC of 292, subtherapeutic. For an elderly patient with chronic kidney disease (CrCl 23 mL/min) it gives an AUC of 2060 with a predicted trough of 75 mg/L, nearly four times the nephrotoxicity ceiling. Only the middle patient with normal renal function is served, at an AUC of 511, which the engine leaves unchanged. For the two failing patients it solves to target: 1750 mg every 12 hours for the augmented clearance patient and 500 mg every 24 hours (AUC 515) for the CKD patient. As the pharmacist who signed off on these, both are regimens I would actually write. Every regimen here comes from covariates alone, with no measured level required, which is exactly the weekend when no trough could be drawn; the Bayesian fitter then sharpens the estimate the moment a level does arrive. The engine turns the lab from a hard dependency into an optional refinement: it doses safely without one and gets tighter with one.

The first day is its own problem. A maintenance regimen alone does not reach target for three to four doses, so the engine leads with a loading dose: 2000 mg for the young normal patient and 1500 mg for the elderly CKD patient, both reaching an initial peak near 36 mg/L. The loaded patient is in the therapeutic band within hours while the maintenance only curve reaches range only after roughly a day, the window a steady state view cannot see and where I used to lose the first 48 hours waiting on a trough. The gradient itself verifies: across all three modules it matches central finite differences to a relative error of 0.000042 for serum creatinine and 0.000075 for dose, with no NaNs, and every continuous optimum lands in the AUC 400 to 600 band.

The centerpiece is restraint. For an acute kidney injury patient near dialysis range (CrCl 12 mL/min) the continuous optimum of 436 mg every 24 hours hits AUC 500 safely, but snapping to the 250 mg grid forces 500 mg, giving an AUC of 574 and a trough of 20.1 mg/L. The guardrail hard blocks it for breaching the 20 mg/L ceiling and routes the case to level guided dosing. This is the feature, not the failure. Every steady state equation assumes steady state, and acute kidney injury is where that breaks, because renal function changes too fast for the model to hold; real stewardship programs exclude it from AUC Bayesian dosing for exactly this reason. A confident predicted trough there would be false precision, and refusing it is what a careful clinician does. The engine also blocks a deliberately unsafe regimen (3000 mg every 8 hours at CrCl 23) on all four grounds at once.

Individualization holds up under bad data. The MAP fitter minimizes squared residuals between measured and predicted levels, weighted by a combined proportional and additive assay error (about 12 percent plus 2 mg/L), plus a population prior penalty weighted by the published between patient variability [V3][V4]. When a patient clears faster into a smaller volume than covariates predict, two levels move the elimination rate error from 0.037 to 0.013 and the volume error from 8.4 L to 4.7 L. When a single trough is corrupted low, the prior holds those errors at 0.020 and 0.2 L, whereas dropping it lets the estimate chase the outlier to 0.063, worse than where it started.

To show the individualization is real and not just plausible, I ran a parameter recovery experiment on 200 virtual patients. Each patient's true elimination rate and volume were drawn from the population between patient variability model, two steady state levels were simulated from those true values with realistic combined assay noise (12 percent plus 2 mg/L), and the fitter recovered the parameters from just those two numbers. From as few as two noisy levels it recovers the true elimination rate at a correlation of 0.95 and the true volume at 0.84, and it pins the quantity that actually drives dosing, the 24 hour exposure, at a correlation of 0.99 with a relative error of 11.5 percent. The population prior alone carried a 48.5 percent exposure error, so two levels cut that roughly fourfold. The prior also earns its keep under bad data: when a single trough is corrupted spuriously low, the elimination rate error stays near 57 percent with the prior on but blows out to 116 percent with it off, because the prior anchors the estimate that a no prior fit chases toward the outlier.

Alongside the locked one compartment core I built a two compartment model as a separate, selectable module behind the identical contract, using an analytic biexponential so its gradients stay clean. It verifies on every count: the loss gradient with respect to dose matches finite differences to 0.0018, the AUC identity holds to 0.0000002, and it recovers the distribution phase the one compartment model collapses, a true peak near 74 mg/L against 26 for the same clearance [V2][V3]. Its four parameter inverse moves the clearance error from 2.06 to 0.04 L/h and the central volume error from 2.25 to 1.69 L while the two unidentifiable parameters stay anchored to the prior. This four parameter fit is the honest home of the framework necessity argument.

## Beyond vancomycin

Vancomycin is the proof, not the point. The reusable, drug agnostic machinery is the differentiable compartment modules, the physiology module, the optimizer, and the Bayesian fitter. Different drugs need different structure, not just different numbers, so generality is a library of composable templates (one compartment linear, two compartment, saturable elimination) run through the same optimizer and guardrails. The objective is swappable too: staying in the therapeutic window is only one loss; the same machinery can maximize efficacy under a toxicity ceiling or minimize fluctuation. A single center implementation of this AUC Bayesian workflow reported target attainment within 48 hours rising from 28 to 72 percent with a fall in acute kidney injury, real world evidence that the approach delivers, with the honest caveat that it is a single center retrospective, not a randomized trial [R1]. The credible claim is not that one model eats every drug, but that the same engine that doses vancomycin can individualize any drug for the patient whose physiology breaks the standard dose.

## Generality: a second drug with a different model structure, not a parameter swap

To show this is reusable infrastructure and not a one drug demo, I ran a second drug, phenytoin, through the same engine. Phenytoin breaks the assumption vancomycin relies on. Vancomycin is eliminated by a first order process, so its steady state has a closed form and the level scales with the dose. Phenytoin is capacity limited, described by Michaelis and Menten, so there is no constant half life and no closed form. As the daily input approaches the maximum metabolic rate Vmax, the level rises steeply. Near saturation a 20 percent dose increase can nearly triple the level and cross into toxicity.

I did not rewrite the engine. I swapped only the two genuinely drug specific parts: the pharmacokinetic module, now a nonlinear differential equation integrated with a fixed step solver in place of the linear superposition, and the clinical loss, now a total serum level of 10 to 20 mg/L in place of the exposure ratio. Everything else was reused unchanged: the same physiology module mapping covariates to body size, the same gradient descent optimizer, and the same Bayesian fitter. Parameters are standard published values: Vmax about 7 mg/kg/day, Km about 4 to 6 mg/L, volume of distribution near 0.65 L/kg (Spruill 2001; Winter).

Verification held to the same bar. Gradients through all three modules matched finite differences to a relative error near three parts in ten thousand, with no NaN, including the gradient through the shared physiology module. The optimizer placed the continuous dose on a trough of 15 mg/L for a normal, a fast, and a slow metabolizer, and the clinical grid landed each in the therapeutic band. For the sparse data case, the two saturable parameters cannot both be read from one blood draw, so I put a tight prior on Km and let a single level inform Vmax. That one draw moved the Vmax estimate from a population error near 4 mg/h to under one tenth of that, with no NaN. The engine that doses vancomycin also individualizes phenytoin.

## Honest limits and future work

The core is a one compartment model with a fixed differentiable infusion time; the reporting layer applies the exact durations, which is exact for AUC and only mildly affects peak and trough. The population priors are the bedside standard for a reason: Cockcroft and Gault and Matzke are the equations clinicians actually use, they are transparent, and Onor showed the population prediction correlates strongly with the patient specific value [C1][V5][V1]. They also have edges, underestimating clearance in the critically ill and the obese, which the Bayesian update from measured levels corrects. The forward looking replacement is a modern population pharmacokinetic prior such as Goti, Colin, or Thomson [P1]; because the physiology module is one service behind a typed contract, that is a substitution, not a rewrite. The two compartment model is built and verified, leaving a dialysis aware clearance model to dose through the window the engine currently refuses. Beyond the saturable elimination case already demonstrated with phenytoin, the same templates extend to effect target drugs such as warfarin dosed to INR, to closed loop settings such as insulin against continuous glucose data, and to modern incretin therapies such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, where titrating to effect while holding down gastrointestinal side effects is itself an optimization problem the same machinery can solve. The scope stays fixed: I build the router, not the maps. The engine optimizes given a model or a few measurements; it does not run trials or validate any drug's parameters, which is the work of pharmacometricians and monitoring services.
